# Operator content reference

What real posts, photos and comments look like on language-exchange apps, as a guide for what the operator-account generators should produce. These are patterns observed by reading feeds; no post text, photo, name or handle from another service is stored here or anywhere in the repo, and generated content must be original.

## HelloTalk posts

**Language.** Most posts are written in the language the author is learning, not their own. A Chinese user's post is usually in Korean: polite textbook endings, slightly off particles and word choice, sometimes stiff written style (`~습니다`) mixed with chat style. Some posts are in the author's own language (a Chinese caption or proverb), some are bilingual with the same line in two languages, and a few are one English line.

**What they are about**, roughly by share:

| Kind | Share | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-introduction and looking for friends or a language partner | about half | Who I am, what I am learning, what I can teach, "please message me". Often names a preference (same gender, lives in Korea, likes K-pop). |
| Photo with a short caption | about a quarter | A few words to one line: a mood, a place, "photo dump", a season remark. |
| Request for recommendations or meetups | one in ten | Places to take photos, where to go alone on a holiday, a clinic, "anyone want to meet near DDP tomorrow". Concrete places. |
| Study life | one in ten | An exam result, how hard listening has become, forgetting the language after going home, asking how to study. |
| Daily remark | a few | Bought glasses today, the wind by the river is strong. |

Common details: a list-style introduction (MBTI, birth year, nationality, height, home city on separate lines); a first-post badge; one to three topic tags under the text (언어교환, 한국어연습, 한국유학, 여행기, a city or neighborhood); emoticons and `~~`, `ㅎㅎ`, `ㅠㅠ` even from learners; length mostly one to four lines, with long posts cut after about five lines.

**Photos on posts.** Nearly every post has photos; text-only posts are rare. Counts seen: one photo (about a third), two or three (about a third), a grid of four to nine (about a third). What they show:

- Selfies and portraits of the author: indoors at home or a cafe, mirror selfies with the phone covering part of the face, full-body outfit shots taken by a friend, close-ups with a beauty filter. This is the majority.
- Sets from one outing: the same person in several poses, mixed with scenery from the same place (sea, pool, sunset, city view, flowers).
- Food on a table, a drink, a dessert.
- Objects and pets: a cat, a plush toy, a handwritten study note, a test score sheet.
- An occasional illustration, sticker or screenshot among the photos.

Photos look like phone photos: uneven light, tilted, cropped heads, night shots with noise, black-and-white filters, one blurry frame left in a set.

**Profile pictures.** Small circles, so only the gist reads: a face close-up or a selfie for most; a full-body or far-away shot for some; a pet, a cartoon character, an illustration or an object for a noticeable minority. A flag badge sits on the picture.

**Comments.** Median about 20 characters; nine in ten are under 70. Kinds: a compliment ("pretty", "nice pics", a row of heart emoji), an answer to the post's question (a list of neighborhoods, "bring a cardigan, it is already cold"), an offer ("talk with me!", "let's be friends"), a short joke or teasing, a correction or encouragement of the learner's writing. Comments come in several languages under one post (Korean, Chinese, English, French). Many posts get an encouraging comment from the app's own AI account. Typical counts: 20 to 150 likes and 1 to 7 comments; a few posts reach several hundred likes.

## HelloTalk: the unfiltered feed (최신) and the help feed (도움)

The recommended feed shows the posts that did well. The newest feed shows what people actually post, and it is plainer:

- **Most posts get almost nothing**: 0 to 3 likes and no comments within the first hour. The 100-like posts of the recommended feed are the exception.
- **Many are in the author's own language**, not the one being learned: a Chinese caption of a few characters, a remark about the sky or the weather, a proverb or a line of song lyric.
- **Many are close to wordless**: an emoticon, two syllables or a single emoji over one or two selfies.
- **Video is common**: a short selfie clip or a clip of the sky or a street, often with a face filter.
- **Other things seen**: a diet remark after overeating, getting braces at the dentist, a pet that clings, not knowing how to explain a haircut to a hairdresser, a family business opening, an invitation to a voice room or a live stream (shown as a card), a cartoon or meme image instead of a photo.
- **Time and distance labels** sit on each post ("53분 전"; in 주변, a neighborhood and "3.4km").

The help feed is language work, and it is where native speakers are useful:

- A learner's paragraph in the target language with a request to correct it, often textbook-like (a paragraph of simple sentences on one topic).
- **Voice posts**: the same paragraph read aloud (20 to 40 seconds) asking how the pronunciation is.
- "What does this mean?" and "what is the difference between these expressions?" questions.
- Bilingual posts with the sentence in two languages.
- Longer personal writing: a reflective paragraph, an aphorism, a short story in English tagged as writing practice.
- Topic tags name the request: 수정해 주세요, 제 발음 어때요?, 이게 무슨 뜻이에요?, 언어 교환, 수다 떨기, 영어 학습.

## Kfriends

A much thinner feed than HelloTalk, and mostly not about language at all.

**Who is there.** In the sample almost nobody was Korean: users from India, Turkey, Arabic-speaking countries, Spanish-speaking countries and Russia, posting in English or Arabic. Language codes under the name (EN, AR, KO, ZH) show what they speak or study; KO appears on some, but no post was written in Korean.

**Story posts.** About one post every one to two minutes across the whole app. Nearly all are text only and one line long:

- A greeting into the void: "hello hello" with repeated letters and emoji, "maybe hi?", a single emoji row.
- Boredom and asking for a chat: "so boring, can I have a chat", "I think nobody is ready to talk with anyone".
- Complaints about the app itself (lagging, "trash app").
- Off-platform pushes: "I'm not active here, follow me on" another service; "DM me" offers.
- A few with an image: a motivational quote card, a music screenshot, a holiday remark with a photo.
- Sexual or provocative bait from a few accounts.
- Occasional `#daily life` tag; otherwise no tags.

Likes are 0 to 4 and comments 0 to 3. Comments are a word or two: "hii", "why", "yes if you want", a pair of emoji. Each post shows who liked it by name and has a comment box directly under it, so replying is one tap.

**Friend list (친구 찾기).** Each row is a profile picture, a name, language codes and a one-line status. Statuses are very short and in English: "hola", "be kind", "just smile", "I am new", a row of emoji, or a Korean phrase from a learner. Names are nicknames, often decorated with symbols, emoji or stylized letters. Filters: all, matched, nearby, gender, country, language, age.

**Home.** Recommended friends as large photo cards (name, flag, language codes, follow and wave buttons), then "friends who match you" grouped by keyword (film, pets, running) and by trait (rational, likes talking, outgoing).

**Notifications.** Almost entirely "X visited your profile" and "X followed you", with a follow-back button; comment and like notices are rare. Profile visits are the main sign of life.

**Profile pictures.** Selfies and portraits, a pet, a flower or scenery, an illustration or anime character, a plain dark image. The same range as HelloTalk but with more non-face pictures.

**What a Kfriends-like feed lacks**, and Mingle can do better: posts in Korean, natives who answer, photos, and replies. An account that writes a real sentence, answers a greeting or asks a question already stands out there.

## Native casual Korean (Threads)

Line break after every clause; note-style endings (`~함`, `~음`, `~는 거임`, `~더라`); stories quote what someone said word for word; posts end with a rhetorical question to invite replies rather than a survey question; popular posts run five to ten lines; `ㅋㅋㅋ` and `ㅠㅠ` freely. A fair share of what search returns is promotion and should not be imitated.

## What this means for the generators

1. **Language of the post.** Accounts whose persona is learning a language should write a share of their posts in that language, at a learner's level. Today every post is in the account's first language. Needs the post language stored per reserve post.
2. **Topic mix.** Raise self-introduction and friend-seeking far above the current weight, add the list-style introduction and the meetup or place-recommendation request, and allow one to three topic tags.
3. **Photos on posts.** A text-only feed is itself unusual. Most posts need one to nine phone-style photos that match the caption; photo sets should repeat the same person and place.
4. **Comments.** Short, mostly compliments, answers and offers to chat, in mixed languages.
5. **Engagement.** Likes and comments on operator posts should be mostly near zero, with an occasional popular post, as on the newest feed.
6. **Native-speaker role.** Korean and Japanese accounts are the natives learners look for: answering "what does this mean", correcting a learner's sentence and replying to introductions is as typical for them as posting.
7. **Profile pictures.** The existing taxonomy (face, partly hidden, from behind, body, object, animal, scenery, several people) covers what was seen; cartoon or illustration avatars are more common than its 1% "other" share.

## Making generated photos look like phone photos

Worked out in October 2026 by drafting profile photos in ChatGPT and comparing them with real phone photos. The rules live in `mingle-app/src/server/operator-avatars/photo-realism.ts` (every photo) and `taxonomy.ts` (profile photos).

What gave drafts away, most noticeable first:

1. **The face lit more brightly than the place.** A face that is evenly and frontally bright in a shaded alley or at night reads as pasted in. The face gets only the light the place gives; a shaded spot with a bright sky behind (face a little dull, sky blown out) looked the most real. Naming a flash at all, even to forbid it, pulled the picture toward a flash look, so prompts never mention it.
2. **Eyes staring into the lens.** A selfie taker looks at their own face on the screen just below the lens. Saying only "slightly below the lens" made the model look away entirely; describing the phone held at eye level and the eyes on the screen worked.
3. **Retouching.** Prompts say the photo comes straight out of the camera app with no beauty app, face slimming, eye enlarging, skin smoothing or added glow, and ask for matte skin with pores and only natural shine.
4. **Hair arranged strand by strand.** A light breeze outdoors (a few loose strands, split bangs, flyaways) and a few flyaways indoors; a strong wind ruined the hairstyle.
5. **Every color matching.** People coordinate their outfits, so the outfit stays coordinated; the background gets a few everyday clashing colors (a neon sign, a blue bin, a red fire extinguisher box, an orange cone).
6. **Contradictions in the prompt.** A selfie that is also a full-body shot, park scenery with window light, film-camera looks next to phone-camera lines: the model averages them into a staged picture. Selfies never get full-body framing, light follows indoor or outdoor, and non-phone looks and color filters were removed.
7. **Every East Asian woman drawn the same way.** Nationality alone gave Korean-looking faces. Each country with a known look (`COUNTRY_LOOKS`) adds its face, makeup, hair trend and dress style; for Japan that meant matte base, blush high under the eyes and across the nose, softly arched brows, a full coral or red-brown lip, milk-tea brown hair with an inward-curled bob or see-through bangs, and loose layered muted clothes.

8. **Every attractive face the same face.** Asked for "beautiful" or "good-looking", the model averages toward big round eyes, a tiny pointed nose, a V-line chin and perfect symmetry; the prettier the request, the more typical the face. What finally worked (`face-anatomy.ts`) came from scoring drafts against a real, untypical beauty (100 = that photo, 0 = an average face):
   - Bending many traits at once made a plain face: six off-ideal traits (wide face, low nose, visible nostrils, big front teeth, short midface, small eyes) scored about 20.
   - Two or three distinctive traits on an otherwise pretty face, with every feature named concretely, scored 70 to 90: a small face with a high, wide forehead and wide-set monolid eyes (90); downturned eyes over a large forehead and a soft lower face (90); broad high cheekbones on a lean short face (80); a wide mouth with soft jaw corners (70). A long face with a high nose looked too long (50).
   - The untypical look often comes from how features sit together, not from any single feature; each feature on its own can be ordinary.
   - "Wide face" is drawn as chubby unless the flesh is lean and the width is said to come from bone.
   - A short, compact face with a short midface, full cheeks that lift and a wide mouth reads as cute and round and scored about 50, laughing or not; the expression did not raise scores, the face shape and features did. These traits are kept out of striking faces.
   - "A small face" with a high forehead, a small chin and hair pulled back without bangs drew an oversized skull above the forehead. Faces are never called small, every face prompt says the head is in natural proportion, and a high forehead always gets bangs or face-framing hair.
   - A lean face with prominent front cheekbones read as masculine (50), and a soft round outline with a slim chin was pretty but plain (70).
   - Ten real faces the founder scores 100 each break the AI beauty in at least one place, mostly the same few: soft youthful fullness in the cheeks over a small, narrow jaw (not hollow model cheeks); a short, rounded chin (not a long V); a small, low or short nose (not high, thin and pointed); a small but full mouth; horizontally long, heavy-lidded, slightly downturned or wide-set eyes (not big, round and double-lidded); a short midface. Fullness only hurt with a broad jaw or a wide mouth.
   - A prompt that spelled out every feature with a "do not …" list scored about 50 even with those reference traits; the model's own face is pretty, and every high score named only two or three traits.
   - Naming only two traits but leaving out beauty words gave a pleasant, plain face (50); a heavy upper lid with a short rounded chin drew the same round face twice. Striking faces now say "exceptionally beautiful … yet not the typical AI beauty" and never get those two traits.
   - The model exaggerates whatever is named: "eyes set noticeably wide apart" and "a full upper lip" came out as staring eyes and filler lips. Traits are now worded mildly and the line reads "subtly unlike a typical pretty face".
   - From one prompt, an evenly lit, smooth-skinned draft scored 85 for beauty but looked typical, and a side-lit draft with real pore texture scored 40. The side light and texture were what looked real and are kept; the 40 came from the face itself, a distinctiveness that took beauty away.
   - Asking in the abstract worked best: "not the typical AI beauty, a distinctive look of her own and still overwhelmingly beautiful; the distinctiveness adds to the beauty, never takes away from it", with no features named, scored 80 to 85 and did not look typical. Striking faces now get exactly that plus a random impression word (cat-like, puppy-like, fox-like, innocent, chic, graceful …) so they do not converge on one face; no traits and no charm point are named for them.
   - An impression word ("fox-like") raised the abstract prompt to 95 but pulled the face back toward the typical one. Saying the proportions are a real person's, "the kind no one would take for an AI-generated face", made the nose and face shape less typical (90); adding one mild trait (slightly wide-set eyes) kept 95 and eased the typical look a little. Striking faces now get one of the two that scored well, at random: the real-person wording with an impression word (90, least typical), or the plain wording with an impression word and one mild trait (95). Single drafts vary a lot: the same prompt gave a fairly untypical face once and a very typical one the next time. Two lines made the face less typical while keeping 95: "the slight natural asymmetry of a real face: one eye a touch smaller or one brow a little higher" and frizzy, static flyaway hair; every striking face now gets both. With the asymmetry in place, the real-person wording only lowered beauty (85) without making the face less typical, so striking faces settled on: a distinctive look in the abstract, an impression word, natural asymmetry and flyaway hair, nothing else about the face. On top of that, "very subtle details of a real face" (three or four from the whole list, each from a different area; forcing one onto the eyes, nose or mouth felt rigid. Bone-structure details (a slightly long face, cheekbones, forehead, chin, midface) made faces clearly less typical, while details that match the model's own cute beauty (a round face, dimples, wide-set eyes with a bubbly impression) were absorbed and changed nothing, so areas are weighted that way. The list also has downturned or slightly small eyes, an inner-fold crease, slightly wide nostrils, a short nose or tiny bump, a thin upper lip or soft lip line, uneven or low brows, slightly protruding ears, faint freckles or moles, faintly visible jaw corners, an uneven hairline) from the founder's list (slightly wide- or close-set eyes, short eye corners, monolids, a slightly long or short midface, unfilled lips or a slightly small mouth, a bridge that stays low between the eyes under a high tip, a pointed or round tip, a slightly round or slightly long face instead of an egg oval, lean cheeks or slightly strong cheekbones, a slightly long or short philtrum, a slightly weak or strong chin, a broad, wide or narrow forehead, thin hair, the faintest smile lines, dimples), at most one per area and always worded "very slightly". Mild eye and lip traits (lifted outer corners, a slightly fuller upper lip) raised beauty to 100 but made the face more typical, and a detailed round-outline prompt fell back to 50.
   So faces are picked axis by axis, as an aesthetic-surgery consult would (face shape, flesh, forehead, cheekbones, lower face, midface, eyelid crease, eye shape, spacing and detail, brows, nose bridge, tip and length, lip fullness, mouth width, lip line, philtrum), plus fringe and hairline. Each option is common, distinctive or plain, and only the distinctive and plain ones are written into the prompt ("face, subtly unlike a typical pretty face: …"): a pleasant face gets one or two distinctive traits, an ordinary face gets plain traits too. Striking faces skip this (see above). Everything else is left to the model. Beauty words stay out except "very pretty, not the typical AI or golden-ratio beauty" for striking faces.
10. **Hair, makeup and outfit change the face.** With the face lines unchanged, a blunt bob, an almost bare face and a football jersey drew a clearly untypical face (80, the least AI-looking yet); long straight hair with see-through bangs, full makeup and a knit cardigan draw the typical idol face, and a bold evening makeup pulled it back toward a "made-up beauty". Bare and minimal makeup are now common, long straight hair is less common, bobs, wolf cuts, perms, claw-clip buns and grown-out roots were added, and the Korean hair and dress notes no longer push one clean, long-haired look.
9. **Light that a real afternoon gives.** Low late-afternoon sun from one side, warm on one side of the face and the wall behind and soft shade on the other, looked as real as the backlit shade.

What did not need fixing: pretty faces (a profile picture is someone's best photo), mushy far background details nobody looks at, and forced flaws such as a fingertip in the frame (people frame their own photos with care).
